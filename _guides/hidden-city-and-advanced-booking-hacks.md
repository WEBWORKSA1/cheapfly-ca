Hidden-city ticketing, open-jaws, positioning flights and split tickets can all cut the price of a trip, but they carry very different levels of risk. Open-jaw and multi-city bookings are fully legitimate and often underused, while hidden-city and self-transfer itineraries can save money only if you understand exactly what can go wrong. This guide walks through each technique the way an experienced fare hunter actually uses it.

## Key takeaways

- **Open-jaw and multi-city** tickets are the safest "hacks": allowed by every airline and often priced close to a simple return.
- **Hidden-city ticketing** breaks most airline contracts of carriage. It only works one-way, with no checked bags and no loyalty number attached.
- **Positioning flights** to a bigger or cheaper hub can unlock much lower long-haul fares, but book them with a generous buffer.
- **Split tickets and self-transfers** offer no protection if you miss a connection, so plan for the worst case before you buy.
- **VPN and cookie tricks** are mostly myth. Point-of-sale differences are real but rarely worth the hassle.

## Hidden-city ticketing: how it works

Airline pricing is based on markets, not distance. Sometimes a flight from A to C via B is cheaper than a nonstop from A to B, because the A–C market is more competitive. A hidden-city traveller books A to C and simply gets off at B.

> **Example:** Imagine a nonstop from Halifax to Toronto is priced higher than a Halifax–Toronto–Chicago itinerary on the same airline, because Chicago has more competition. A traveller who only wants Toronto books to Chicago and walks out of the airport at Pearson. The numbers here are illustrative, not a live fare.

Search tools that specialize in this exist, and Google Flights occasionally surfaces such itineraries by accident. The saving can be real, but so are the strings attached.

### The rules you would be breaking

Most airlines, including Canada's largest carriers and the major US airlines, prohibit "point beyond" or hidden-city ticketing in their tariffs and conditions of carriage. The usual remedies are cancelling any remaining flights on the booking, charging the difference between what you paid and the fare you actually flew, and removing points or closing your frequent-flyer account. Read the current tariff on the airline's website before deciding, because the wording changes.

> **Heads up:** This is general information, not legal advice. Breaking a contract of carriage is a civil matter, not a crime, but the airline decides how to enforce it.

### The practical risks

- **Checked bags go to the final destination.** Never check a bag. On a full flight, even a carry-on can be gate-checked through to the end city.
- **It only works one-way.** Skip a segment and the airline will usually cancel every remaining flight on the booking, including your return.
- **Irregular operations ruin it.** If the airline reroutes you through a different hub after a delay, you may land nowhere near your real destination.
- **Repeat patterns get noticed.** Doing it often on the same airline, especially with your loyalty number attached, is the fastest way to trigger enforcement.
- **Basic fares make it harder.** Many cheap fares on Canadian airlines now include only a personal item, so travel light; our [carry-on packing guide]({{ '/guides/carry-on-only-packing-guide/' | relative_url }}) shows how.

## Open-jaw tickets: the legitimate cousin

An open-jaw flies you into one city and home from another. Toronto to London, then Rome to Toronto, is a classic Europe example. You cover the gap by train, bus or a cheap regional flight, and you avoid paying to backtrack.

Airlines usually price an open-jaw as half of each return fare added together, so it often costs about the same as a round trip to either city. Sometimes it is cheaper, because you can fly home from the city with the weaker demand. Use the "multi-city" option on the airline's site or any search engine to build one.

## Multi-city itineraries and stopovers

Multi-city bookings go a step further, letting you stitch three or more flights onto one ticket. Done well, you can add an extra city for little or no extra fare, especially with airlines that run formal programs. Our guide to [airline stopover programs]({{ '/guides/stopover-programs-free-extra-destination/' | relative_url }}) covers which carriers let Canadians spend several nights in their hub at no extra airfare.

The big advantage over self-built itineraries is protection: everything is on one ticket, so if the first flight is delayed, the airline must get you to the end of your journey.

## Positioning flights

A positioning flight (or drive) gets you to a different departure airport where long-haul fares are cheaper. Common Canadian examples include:

- Flying from a smaller city such as Kelowna or Regina to Vancouver or Calgary to catch a cheaper long-haul fare.
- Driving from Ontario, Quebec or BC to a US border airport, which our guide to [flying from US border airports]({{ '/guides/fly-from-us-border-airports/' | relative_url }}) covers in detail.
- Taking a cheap low-cost carrier flight to Toronto or Montréal for a European sale fare.

Because the positioning flight is usually a separate ticket, treat it as a self-transfer (see below). For long-haul departures, arriving the day before is the safest choice.

## Split tickets and self-transfers

Split ticketing means booking two or more separate tickets that together are cheaper than one through-fare. Some booking sites build these automatically and label them as "self-transfer" or as combinations of one-way fares.

The saving can be large, but you take on all the connection risk. If your first flight is late and you miss the second, the second airline usually treats you as a no-show. Canada's passenger-protection rules apply ticket by ticket, so the first airline is not responsible for a separate onward booking; our [APPR compensation guide]({{ '/guides/canada-air-passenger-rights-appr-compensation/' | relative_url }}) explains what is covered.

| Technique | Main benefit | Main risk | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open-jaw | No backtracking, similar price | Minimal | Multi-country trips |
| Multi-city / stopover | Extra city for little extra | Fare rules vary | Long-haul holidays |
| Positioning flight | Access cheaper hubs | Missed long-haul departure | Travellers outside big hubs |
| Split tickets | Lower total fare | No missed-connection protection | Flexible, carry-on travellers |
| Hidden-city | Lower one-way fare | Contract breach, lost bags, cancelled returns | Rare one-way trips only |

### Rules for safer self-transfers

1. Leave a large buffer: at least three hours for domestic connections and five or more for international ones, more in winter.
2. Remember that you must collect and re-check bags, and possibly clear immigration, which means you need any entry authorization that country requires.
3. Look for travel insurance that explicitly covers missed connections on separate tickets.
4. Avoid the last flight of the day on the first leg.

## VPNs, cookies and point of sale: myths vs reality

**Myth: searching repeatedly makes prices rise because of cookies.** Fares change because cheaper fare buckets sell out and search results are cached, not because a site remembers you. Clearing cookies rarely changes anything.

**Reality: point of sale can matter.** An airline's website for another country, or a booking site in another market, may display different fares, currencies or promotions. Occasionally that produces a genuine saving.

**Reality check on the saving:** Most Canadian credit cards add a foreign transaction fee, commonly around 2.5 percent, when you pay in another currency. Refunds come back in that currency, and customer service may be run from abroad. Some fares are also restricted to residents of the selling country.

## A quick decision checklist

1. Is the saving large enough to justify the risk? On a short domestic hop, it rarely is.
2. Can you travel with only a personal item or a small carry-on?
3. Is this a one-way trip, or the final leg of your journey?
4. Would a missed connection leave you stranded, or just inconvenienced?
5. Could an open-jaw or multi-city ticket get you most of the saving with full protection?

## Bottom line

Open-jaw, multi-city and positioning strategies are legitimate tools every Canadian fare hunter should use. Split tickets can work for flexible travellers who plan generous buffers. Hidden-city ticketing is a high-risk, one-way, carry-on-only tactic that breaks airline rules, so treat it as a last resort rather than a habit.
