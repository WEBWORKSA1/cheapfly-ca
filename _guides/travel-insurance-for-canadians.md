Your provincial or territorial health plan pays only a small part of emergency medical costs outside Canada, and in some cases nothing at all. That is why private emergency medical insurance is the one coverage almost every Canadian traveller needs, with trip cancellation and interruption coverage a strong second for expensive or non-refundable trips. This guide explains what to buy, what the fine print really means and where credit-card coverage falls short.

> **Heads up:** This article is general information, not professional insurance, medical or financial advice. Always read your own policy wording and ask the insurer when in doubt.

## Key takeaways

- **Provincial coverage abroad is minimal**, and a single hospital stay outside Canada, especially in the US, can cost tens of thousands of dollars.
- **Emergency medical insurance** is the priority for every trip outside your province.
- **Trip cancellation and interruption** coverage protects prepaid costs if you cannot travel or must come home early.
- **Credit-card insurance** can be useful but often has age limits, trip-length caps and payment conditions.
- **Pre-existing conditions** must usually be stable for a set period before you leave.

## What provincial health plans cover abroad

Each province and territory runs its own plan, and the rules differ. In general:

- **Payments are limited** to what the same service would cost at home, or to modest daily amounts, which is a small fraction of foreign hospital prices.
- **Ontario discontinued its out-of-country emergency program in 2020**, keeping only a limited benefit for dialysis.
- **Ambulances, air evacuation and repatriation** are generally not covered.
- **Residency rules apply.** You must meet your province's physical-presence rules to stay covered, which matters for long trips and snowbirds.
- **Travel within Canada** is usually covered for medically necessary care, but not everything (such as ambulance costs) is billed the same way between provinces.

The Government of Canada's travel site ([travel.gc.ca](https://travel.gc.ca/)) recommends buying travel insurance before you leave, and you should check your own provincial ministry's website for current limits.

## The main types of coverage

| Coverage | What it typically pays for | Who needs it |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency medical | Hospital, doctors, ambulance, medical evacuation and return to Canada | Everyone leaving their province, especially to the US |
| Trip cancellation | Prepaid, non-refundable costs if a covered reason stops you from leaving | Anyone with expensive or non-refundable bookings |
| Trip interruption | Extra costs to return home early or catch up to your trip | Longer or costly trips |
| Delay and missed connection | Meals, hotels and rebooking costs after covered delays | Travellers on tight or separate-ticket connections |
| Baggage | Lost, stolen or delayed luggage | Travellers with valuable belongings |
| Rental car damage | Damage to a rental vehicle | Drivers who decline the rental company's waiver |

**Cancel for any reason** add-ons exist with some insurers, refunding a portion of prepaid costs for reasons the standard policy excludes, but they cost more and have strict conditions.

## Credit-card coverage: useful but full of fine print

Many Canadian credit cards include travel insurance, and for some travellers it is enough. Before you rely on it, check:

- **Age limits.** Medical coverage often ends or shortens for older cardholders, commonly around age 65.
- **Trip-length caps.** Coverage may last only a set number of days, sometimes shorter for older travellers, and the whole trip may be uncovered if it runs longer.
- **Payment rules.** You may need to charge the full trip, or at least part of it, to the card. Trips booked with points can be treated differently.
- **Benefit maximums.** Cancellation and interruption limits per person may be lower than the cost of your trip.
- **Who is covered.** Spouses and dependent children may or may not be included.
- **Primary or secondary.** Some card coverage pays only after any workplace or other plan.

## Pre-existing conditions and the stability clause

Most policies only cover a pre-existing condition if it has been **stable** for a set period before departure, often 90 or 180 days, sometimes longer for older travellers.

A condition may be considered unstable if, during that period, you had:

- New symptoms or a worsening of existing ones
- A new diagnosis or a referral for tests whose results are pending
- A change in medication or dosage (some routine adjustments may be excluded; read the definition)
- A hospital stay or emergency visit for that condition

If a policy uses a medical questionnaire, answer every question accurately. An incorrect answer can void coverage for the entire claim, even for unrelated issues.

## Trip cancellation: when and why to buy

Buy cancellation coverage at the time of booking, or within any short window your policy allows after your first deposit. Buying weeks later can leave a gap.

Covered reasons usually include serious illness or death of you or a close family member, and certain official travel advisories issued after you book. Common exclusions include changing your mind, events that were known or forecast when you bought the policy (such as a named storm), and fear of travel.

Insurance and passenger rights work together. Canada's Air Passenger Protection Regulations govern what airlines owe you for many delays and cancellations; our [APPR compensation guide]({{ '/guides/canada-air-passenger-rights-appr-compensation/' | relative_url }}) explains those rights. Insurance can then cover losses the airline does not, such as a non-refundable resort night.

## How much coverage is enough?

For emergency medical insurance, look for a high overall maximum. Many Canadian policies offer limits in the millions of dollars, which is appropriate given the cost of hospital care and medical evacuation abroad, especially in the US. Low maximums can look attractive on price but leave you exposed on a serious claim.

For trip cancellation, the limit should at least match the non-refundable cost of your trip per person. If you add expensive non-refundable items later, such as tours or a cruise, check whether you need to increase your coverage.

Deductibles lower your premium but increase what you pay on a claim. A moderate deductible can be sensible for healthy travellers on short trips; a very high one defeats the purpose for many people.

Finally, look for an insurer with a 24/7 assistance service that can arrange direct payment to hospitals. Paying a large bill upfront and waiting for reimbursement can be a serious financial strain.

## Snowbirds and long trips

If you leave Canada several times a year, an **annual multi-trip plan** can be cheaper than buying single-trip policies. Check the maximum length per trip, since many plans cap each trip at a set number of days, with top-up coverage available for longer stays.

For winter-long stays, confirm both your provincial residency rules and your insurer's maximum trip length before you book anything non-refundable.

## A pre-departure checklist

1. Confirm emergency medical coverage for every traveller and every day of the trip.
2. Check the benefit maximum and any deductible.
3. Read the pre-existing condition clause and stability period.
4. Note exclusions: high-risk activities, alcohol-related incidents, pregnancy limits and travel to regions under official advisories.
5. Save the 24/7 assistance contact details and your policy number.
6. Keep receipts for everything; claims need documentation.

> **Example:** A couple booking a sun package in September buys cancellation coverage the same day. If a storm is named a week later and a covered event follows, such as an official advisory against travel, their policy can respond. A friend who buys coverage after the storm is named will typically find that storm excluded.

## Bottom line

Never leave your province without emergency medical coverage, because provincial plans pay very little abroad. Add cancellation and interruption coverage for costly or non-refundable trips, read the stability clause carefully if you have any medical conditions, and treat credit-card insurance as helpful but not automatic.
