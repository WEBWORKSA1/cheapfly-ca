Driving to a US airport near the border can save Canadians money, but only when the fare gap is big enough to cover parking, fuel, exchange-rate costs and extra travel time. It tends to work best for families and groups heading to US leisure destinations, and least well for solo travellers on short trips.

This guide covers the main border airports, a worked true-cost example, the hidden risks, the documents you need and how to decide whether the drive is worth it.

## Quick answer

- **Best candidates:** families or groups flying to US destinations such as Florida, Las Vegas or Arizona, where fares out of Canada are high.
- **Main airports:** Buffalo and Niagara Falls (Toronto and Niagara), Detroit (Windsor and southwestern Ontario), Bellingham and Seattle (Vancouver), Plattsburgh and Burlington (Montréal), Ogdensburg (Ottawa) and Grand Forks or Fargo (Winnipeg).
- **Hidden costs:** parking, fuel, tolls, foreign transaction fees, extra hours and the risk of border delays.
- **Documents:** a valid passport is the simplest, safest choice.
- **Rights:** Canada's passenger protection rules don't cover flights departing from the US.

## Why US airports can be cheaper

US airports often have more airlines competing on leisure routes, including low-cost carriers that don't fly from Canada. Government charges on a US domestic ticket can also be lower than the security charge and airport improvement fees added to Canadian departures. On busy sun routes, that combination can produce a meaningful per-person gap.

It isn't a one-way street, though. US fares are priced in American dollars, so a weak loonie shrinks the advantage. With the Canadian dollar trading at roughly 1.40 to 1.43 per US dollar through much of fall 2026, a US$300 fare costs well over $400 in Canadian money. Several border airports have also trimmed flights aimed at Canadians since cross-border travel dropped in 2025, so options may be thinner than they once were.

## The true-cost math

Here's an illustrative example for a family of four in Toronto flying to Orlando for a week. The fares are hypothetical, not live prices; plug in your own numbers. We've assumed an exchange rate of 1.40 and a card that charges the typical foreign transaction fee of about 2.5%.

| Cost item | From Toronto Pearson | From Buffalo |
|---|---|---|
| Round-trip airfare, 4 travellers | $2,080 ($520 each) | $1,456 (US$1,040) |
| Foreign transaction fee on card | $0 | $36 |
| Getting to the airport | $150 (rideshare or parking) | $60 (fuel and bridge tolls) |
| Airport parking, 8 days | Included above | $170 (about US$120) |
| Total | $2,230 | $1,722 |
| Extra travel time | None | About 4–6 h round trip, plus border waits |

In this example the family saves around $500, which many would happily trade for a few hours in the car. Now run the same fares for one person: the fare gap drops to $156, and after fuel, parking and card fees the net saving shrinks to well under $100 for the same four to six extra hours. For a solo traveller, the drive rarely pays.

## Hidden costs and risks

- **Border waits.** Long lines on holiday weekends can turn a 90-minute drive into three hours. Leave a generous buffer.
- **Winter weather.** Lake-effect snow around Buffalo and mountain passes in the West can slow the drive and delay flights.
- **Early departures.** A 6 a.m. flight may mean a hotel night near the airport, which can erase your savings.
- **Bag fees.** US low-cost carriers charge for carry-ons and checked bags too, so compare total costs.
- **Passenger rights.** The APPR don't apply to flights departing the US. US rules on refunds for cancellations apply instead; see our [APPR compensation guide]({{ '/guides/canada-air-passenger-rights-appr-compensation/' | relative_url }}) for what Canadian departures offer.
- **Health coverage.** Provincial health plans cover very little in the US. Buy travel medical insurance for every trip south of the border.

## Documents you'll need

- **Passport.** A valid Canadian passport is the simplest option. You need it to fly to any international destination, and it works at the land border.
- **NEXUS card.** Speeds up the land crossing and is accepted as ID for US domestic flights. Carry your passport too if your flight leaves the US.
- **Children's documents.** Kids need their own passports to fly. If a child travels with only one parent, the Government of Canada recommends carrying a consent letter from the other parent; see [travel.gc.ca](https://travel.gc.ca) for guidance.
- **Long stays.** Since 2025, the US has required many visitors aged 14 and older who stay 30 days or more to register if they weren't issued an entry record. This mainly affects snowbirds; check current US guidance.
- **Biometrics.** Since late 2025, US authorities photograph non-citizens on entry and exit, so expect a quick facial scan.

When you drive home, remember your personal exemption limits and declare everything you bought. The [Canada Border Services Agency](https://www.cbsa-asfc.gc.ca) lists current allowances.

> **Example:** A couple from Surrey compares Vancouver to Las Vegas with Bellingham to Las Vegas. The Bellingham fare is $90 cheaper per person after conversion. After about $40 in fuel, $70 in parking for four nights and a small card fee, they save roughly $60 total. That's not worth a possible border delay to them, so they fly from Vancouver.

## How to decide

1. Price your trip from your home airport and from the nearest US airport for the same dates.
2. Convert the US fare to Canadian dollars at today's rate.
3. Add fuel, tolls, parking, card fees and any hotel night.
4. Compare total costs, then decide what your extra hours are worth.
5. Check other Canadian alternatives too, such as Hamilton for the Toronto area, before committing to a border run.
