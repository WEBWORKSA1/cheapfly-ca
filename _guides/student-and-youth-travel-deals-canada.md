Canadian students and young travellers can cut travel costs with discount cards, smart routing and well-timed bookings, even though true student airfares are less common than they used to be. The biggest savings come from flexible timing, travelling light and planning big trips such as a gap year or working holiday well in advance. Here is how to make a student budget stretch further.

## Key takeaways

- **Get an internationally recognized student card** such as ISIC if you travel abroad; your campus card often works at home.
- **Dedicated student airfares are rare**; sale fares, flexible dates and smart routing usually beat them.
- **Reading week and summer** are peak times, so book early or travel just outside them.
- **Working holidays abroad** are open to many young Canadians through youth mobility arrangements, with age limits that vary by country.
- **A 12-month plan** gives you time to save, secure visas and catch the best fares.

## Student ID cards worth having

**ISIC (International Student Identity Card).** The best-known international student ID, recognized by attractions, museums and some transport providers worldwide. In Canada, the digital card has cost about 20 dollars a year at last check, with a plastic card available for a small extra fee. Prices change, so confirm on the official ISIC Canada site.

The ISIC family also includes cards for young non-students and for teachers, which can unlock some of the same discounts.

**Your campus card.** Within Canada, many museums, theatres and transit systems accept a valid university or college card. Abroad, it may or may not be accepted, which is where ISIC helps.

**Retail discount cards.** Canadian student discount programs focus on shopping and dining rather than travel, but can still help with luggage, gear and pre-trip costs.

> **Pro tip:** Carry both your ISIC and your campus card. Some places only recognize one, and the cost of either is quickly recovered on a single museum or rail discount.

## Do student or youth airfares still exist?

Dedicated student and youth fares have mostly faded on mainstream Canadian routes. Where they still exist, they are usually sold through specialist student travel agencies on international routes, and the benefit is often flexibility, such as a cheaper date change or an extra bag, rather than a lower headline price.

Always compare with regular sale fares. A flash sale on a full-service airline or a low-cost carrier fare will frequently beat a student fare on price alone.

Youth discounts are more common on the ground. Rail passes in Europe, such as Eurail and Interrail, offer discounted youth pricing for travellers under a set age cut-off, and many city transit systems and attractions have youth or student rates.

## Budget routing for students

### Getting to Europe

- **Watch the shoulder seasons.** Late April to early June and September to October usually bring better transatlantic fares than July and August.
- **Use gateway cities.** Reykjavik, Dublin and Lisbon are often good-value entry points; see our [Reykjavik]({{ '/destinations/reykjavik/' | relative_url }}) and [Lisbon]({{ '/destinations/lisbon/' | relative_url }}) pages.
- **Book open-jaw tickets** to fly into one city and home from another, and fill the gap by train or budget airline.
- **Pack for budget airlines.** European low-cost carriers can be very cheap if you travel with only a small under-seat bag.

### Getting around Canada and the US

- **Compare nearby airports**, including US border airports for travellers in southern Ontario, Quebec and BC.
- **Fly midweek** and avoid Friday and Sunday departures around reading week and holidays.
- **Use one-way fares** to mix airlines when you are heading home for the holidays.

### Getting home for reading week and the holidays

Trips home at Thanksgiving, the winter break and reading week are where many students overspend, because everyone flies on the same days.

- **Book holiday flights home early**, ideally as soon as your exam schedule is confirmed. The cheapest fares on peak days disappear first.
- **Shift by a day.** Leaving the morning after your last exam, or returning a day before classes, often costs less than the obvious Friday and Sunday flights.
- **Consider the bus or train** for shorter corridors, such as Toronto, Ottawa, Montréal and Québec City, where ground options can rival flights once airport time is included.
- **Share rides to the airport** or use transit to avoid parking and ride-hail costs.

## Working holidays abroad: the basics

Canada has youth mobility arrangements with dozens of countries and territories. Foreign youth use International Experience Canada to work here, and in return young Canadians can apply for working holiday or youth mobility visas in partner countries.

The general shape of these arrangements:

- **Age limits** are usually 18 to 30 or 18 to 35, depending on the country.
- **Length of stay** is typically 12 to 24 months, and some arrangements allow longer or a second participation.
- **You apply through the partner country**, not through the Canadian government, and quotas can fill up.
- **Common requirements** include proof of funds, health insurance for the whole stay, and either a return ticket or enough money to buy one.
- **Work restrictions** vary; some countries limit certain types of jobs.

Start with the Government of Canada's official travel site ([travel.gc.ca](https://travel.gc.ca/)), then confirm details on the partner country's official immigration website.

> **Heads up:** This is general information, not immigration advice. Visa rules, ages and quotas change, so rely on official sources before you apply.

## Money tips for young travellers

- **Avoid foreign transaction fees.** Many Canadian cards add around 2.5 percent on foreign purchases; a no-foreign-fee card or a low-fee account can save a lot over a long trip.
- **Check your student health plan.** Some campus plans include limited travel coverage, but rarely enough for a long trip, so read the details.
- **Book hostels and shared stays early** for summer in Europe.
- **Use an eSIM or local SIM** instead of roaming.
- **Cook some meals** and buy groceries; it is the easiest daily saving.
- **Keep an emergency buffer.** Set aside enough for a last-minute flight home, kept separate from your daily budget, so an unexpected change of plans does not become a crisis.
- **Volunteer or work-exchange carefully.** Programs that trade a few hours of work for a bed can cut costs, but confirm they are legitimate and allowed under the visa you hold.

> **Example:** A student flying Toronto to Lisbon in late September, travelling by train and budget airline through Spain and Italy, then flying home from Rome on an open-jaw ticket, avoids both peak summer fares and a costly return to Lisbon.

Student travel savings in Canada come less from special fares and more from flexible timing, light packing and smart routing. Get an ISIC card if you are going abroad, plan big trips a year ahead, and check official sources for any working holiday you are considering.
